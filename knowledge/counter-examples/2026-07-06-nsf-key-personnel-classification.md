## Prompt

Sam emailed Bill and Nick (Riley cc'd) explaining that Riley's proposal to an NSF solicitation lists Nick as Key Personnel, and asking Nick and Bill to confirm the classification. The constraint Sam laid out: NSF limits Key/Senior Personnel to no more than 2 months of salary per year cumulatively across all of the individual's NSF proposed and awarded activities; once named Key Personnel on any NSF proposal, the person must remain Key on all future NSF proposals. Sam's guidance: if Nick expects to charge more time to awards, Other Personnel is better (allows exceeding 2 months); if Nick expects limited time and his role is the scientific/technical direction, Key is better. Sam asked Nick to confirm Key or Other.

Nick asked for a drafted reply. After a clarifying exchange about which classification he wanted — the decision being his, since it turns on expected NSF effort — Nick answered "Key." Claude then produced the draft below.

## Claude's draft

Hi Sam,

I'd like to be named as Key Personnel on Riley's proposal. I understand this caps me at 2 months of salary per year across all NSF activities and carries forward to any future NSF proposals — that works for me.

Thanks,
Nick

## My revision

Hi Sam,

I think Key Personnel makes sense for this and moving forward. I won't be spending time doing any technical work on this proposal and will most likely attend meetings and provide some architectural direction. This would also be the most likely scenario for any future proposals I am involved in as well. This would make the amount of time I charge to awarded activities pretty low and even if I were to be involved in multiple awarded projects I believe I would still be able to stay under the 2-months restriction.

I will let others chime in if they think this is off base. If I understand this all correctly I think being named Key Personnel now and in the future will work fine with my role.

Thanks,
Nick

## What changed

- The confirmation is the reasoning, not just the verdict. Claude stated the choice and stopped; Nick leads with the call and then explains why Key fits — no technical work, attending meetings and providing architectural direction, low effort charged. For a decision-confirmation email, the "why" is the substance, not an add-on.
- Applied the rule to his situation instead of reciting it back. Claude echoed the NSF constraint to the sender ("caps me at 2 months... carries forward"). Nick doesn't restate policy as policy — he reasons through how it lands for him: low effort means he stays under the 2-month cap even across multiple awards.
- Longer sentences with stacked clauses doing real work, not short clipped ones (voice-guide match).
- Left the door open for pushback — "I will let others chime in if they think this is off base." Claude's draft closed without inviting Bill or Riley to weigh in.
- Hedged his own understanding rather than projecting certainty — "If I understand this all correctly." Claude's "that works for me" was more definitive than Nick would be about rules he's reasoning through.
- The rationale belonged in the draft, not a footnote. Claude offered the role/effort reasoning as an optional note below the draft; Nick made exactly that reasoning the core of the message. When a decision turns on the sender's own reasoning, ask for it and draft it in — the specifics (no technical work, meetings, architectural direction) can't be invented, so the move is to ask, not to relegate to a note.
